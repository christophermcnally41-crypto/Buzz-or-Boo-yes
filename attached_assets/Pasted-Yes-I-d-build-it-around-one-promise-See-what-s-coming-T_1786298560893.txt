Yes. I’d build it around one promise: **“See what’s coming.”** The product should feel more like a beautifully designed cultural intelligence app than a betting app.

## 1. The startup concept

**Working concept:** a visual forecasting platform where people predict what happens next in **Style, Home, City, Real Estate, Weather, and Culture**.

Start with **Boston + Home + Fashion**. Give every user a virtual balance—say **10,000 Forecast Points**—with no cash value. They make predictions, build a track record, compete on leaderboards, and develop expertise scores.

The platform gets something potentially much more valuable: a continuously updating dataset about what people believe will happen next.

Your core flywheel becomes:

**Great questions → predictions → better forecasts → reputation → more users → better data → valuable trend intelligence.**

## 2. What I’d call it

I would avoid names containing “bet,” “wager,” “odds,” or even “prediction market.” They push the brand toward gambling.

Names I’d explore include **NEXT**, **FORA**, **Ahead**, **FWD**, **Before**, **Signal**, **Likely**, **Forecast**, **NextUp**, and **TMRW**.

My favorite conceptual direction is something like **AHEAD**:

> **AHEAD**
> *See what's coming.*

Then:

**AHEAD / STYLE**
**AHEAD / HOME**
**AHEAD / BOSTON**
**AHEAD / PROPERTY**

The trademark/domain situation would obviously need checking before choosing anything.

## 4. Don't make the first version trade like Kalshi

This is an important change I'd make.

For MVP, I wouldn't even require users to understand buying contracts at 37¢.

Ask:

> **What's the probability this happens?**

They move a slider:

**0 ——— 63% ——— 100**

Then:

**How confident are you?**

Low / Medium / High

Later, advanced users could use a market-style interface.

This makes the initial experience understandable to virtually anyone.

## 5. Your scoring system

This could become one of your intellectual-property advantages.

Don't simply give somebody +10 points for being correct.

Prediction accuracy should matter.

Someone who says:

> 51% chance

and gets it right shouldn't receive the same forecasting credit as someone who correctly said:

> 91%.

But someone who constantly says 99% and gets burned should be punished heavily.

There are established forecasting methods such as **Brier scores** that measure probabilistic accuracy.

So someone's profile could eventually say:

**CHRISTOPHER**

Overall Forecaster
**Top 4%**

🏠 Home — **Elite**
👗 Style — **Expert**
🏙 Boston — **Expert**
🌦 Weather — **Developing**

**Accuracy:** 73%
**Calibration:** 89/100
**Resolved:** 487

That becomes addictive without needing cash gambling.

People want to prove:

> **I know what's coming.**

## 9. AI should be underneath everything

But don't market it as another “AI app.”

AI could help turn sloppy user submissions into objectively measurable questions.

User submits:

> “Chrome is totally coming back.”

The system proposes:

> **Will U.S. Google search interest for “chrome bathroom fixtures” exceed “brass bathroom fixtures” for four consecutive weeks before December 31, 2027?**

Then it identifies:

**Resolution source:** Google Trends
**Closing date:** Dec. 31, 2027
**Outcome:** YES/NO

Human moderation approves it.

That's powerful.

## 11. The business model

I wouldn't rely on advertising initially.

You potentially have four revenue businesses inside one company.

**Consumer Pro — $8–15/month:** advanced forecasts, historical data, category dashboards, expert forecasters, neighborhood trends and alerts.

**B2B Trend Intelligence:** brands, designers, retailers, developers and agencies pay considerably more for forecasting dashboards.

**Custom research:** a retailer could ask, “Which of these eight upholstery colors has the greatest probability of consumer growth?” You run a forecasting panel.

**Eventually, regulated real-money markets:** potentially through appropriately regulated partners rather than trying to become an exchange on day one.

That last part should come *much* later and only after specialized legal/regulatory work.

## 15. Your first 90 days

**Days 1–15: Product definition.** Name, positioning, visual identity, market rules, categories, scoring system and initial 50 questions.

**Days 16–30: Prototype.** Design the screens and build a clickable prototype. Put it in front of 20–30 people and watch where they get confused.

**Days 31–60: MVP development.** Build authentication, prediction engine, feeds, profiles, leaderboards, admin tools and market resolution.

**Days 61–75: Private Boston beta.** Invite roughly 100–300 people: designers, fashion students, realtors, restaurant people, creatives, college students and local personalities.

**Days 76–90: Public beta.** Launch Boston publicly, measure retention and learn which markets actually bring people back.

The metric I'd obsess over initially isn't downloads.

It's:

### **What percentage of people who make predictions this week come back next week to see what changed?**

That's the behavior that determines whether you've built a business.
